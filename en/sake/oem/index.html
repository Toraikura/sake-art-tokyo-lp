SAKE OEM

OEM sake in Japan.
Look at the scope.

A search for “sake OEM” can lead to very different kinds of production.

Some breweries use OEM for an existing sake with custom packaging. Others use it for contract brewing that includes ingredients or liquid design. The useful first question is not “What does OEM mean?” but “How much of the product do I want to change?”

Alcohol production in Japan requires a manufacturing licence under the Liquor Tax Act. The actual producer is therefore the licensed brewery, not simply the company or brand commissioning the project.

BEFORE THE BREWERY

Decide seven things
before you ask.

A brewery can respond much more concretely when these seven points are already clear.

01 / PURPOSE

Why are you making it?

A restaurant house sake, an anniversary bottle, an event, a retail brand or an export product all lead to different requirements.

02 / QUANTITY

How many bottles?

State the target quantity and bottle size, and whether it is a one-off run or something you hope to repeat.

03 / BUDGET

What price can work?

Work backwards from the intended selling price, including bottle, label, box, design, storage and logistics as well as the sake.

04 / SCHEDULE

When do you need it?

Sake is not always available for production on demand. Brewing season and lead time need to be discussed early.

05 / LIQUID

How much should the liquid change?

Is an existing sake acceptable, or do you want to work on sweetness, acidity, aroma, alcohol level and pasteurization?

06 / DESIGN

How far does the brand go?

Label only, or also bottle, cap, box, naming and story? Packaging materials can strongly affect minimum quantities.

07 / ROUTE

Where will it be sold?

Restaurant service, retail, ecommerce and export can require different licensing and logistics checks.

SMALL LOT / COST

Small lot, cost,
lead time.

There is no single nationwide answer to “What is the minimum order for sake OEM?”

Some breweries offer custom-label projects in quantities of only a few dozen bottles. A new brew designed from the rice or fermentation stage may instead require production in the hundreds of litres.

Cost also extends beyond the liquid: packaging, design, plates or printing setup, cartons, storage and shipping can all matter. A better starting point than “the smallest possible lot” is the intended selling price and realistic sales volume.

SMALL LOT ≠ FULL CUSTOMA smaller order does not necessarily mean greater freedom. If the goal is to start small, narrowing the customisation to an existing sake and packaging can be the more realistic route.

OKEGAI

What is okegai?
Is it “buying a barrel”?

In the Japanese sake industry, okegai does not mean buying a wooden barrel of sake.

The Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association defines oketorihiki as a transaction in which alcohol, mainly undiluted sake, is traded between manufacturers without being packed into retail containers. The seller is doing okeuri; the buyer is doing okegai.

That is different from the everyday idea of buying a full decorative barrel or taruzake. For an original sake project, okegai should not be treated simply as a way to buy a tiny batch from a brewery.
